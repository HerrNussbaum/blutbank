# 1 General provisions
# 1.1 Purpose of the tournament rulebook
The Bluthelden® tournament rulebook establishes clear definitions of game mechanics and the resulting rulings. It also regulates tournament operation, the correction of rules violations, and the handling of repeated violations through to deliberate violations (Cheating).
# 1.2 Scope: official tournaments, league games and online events
This rulebook is the reference for all Bluthelden® rules questions. Compliance is mandatory in official tournaments. An event that does not apply this rulebook, or applies only part of it, cannot be an official Bluthelden® tournament or be sanctioned for earning Organized Play points.
# 1.3 Binding card text
The official text of each Bluthelden® card is provided in the card overview (spoiler) at www.bluthelden.com/pages/Kartenspoiler for its set. This text is binding in tournament games.
# 1.4 Card text versus rule text
If card text contradicts or suspends a valid rule, the card text takes precedence over the rulebook.
# 2 Game overview: objectives, card types, zones and materials
This chapter explains the objective of the game, the materials used to play and the game zones.
# 2.1 Objectives and victory conditions
Normal victory conditions
The game ends when one of the following conditions is met, as a Gamestate Check (section 4.10):
1. A Sovereign has 0 life points. The player controlling that Sovereign loses; their opponent wins.
2. A player must draw a card, but the deck they must draw from (Spell Deck or Pool Deck) is empty. That player loses; their opponent wins.
If either condition occurs for both players simultaneously, the game is a draw.

Other victory conditions
An alternative victory condition stated on a card applies when its condition is fulfilled and it has resolved through the Reaction Chain.
The game is also a draw if an uninterruptible sequence of mandatory effects makes normal continuation impossible (an infinite loop).
# 2.2 Card categories and card types
# 2.2.1 Card use and tournament legality
Cards are tournament-legal only if the following requirements are met:
• Only original Bluthelden cards may be used.
• Digital cards are allowed only on officially sanctioned Bluthelden® platforms.
• Proxies are not allowed in official tournaments. Tournament management may provide replacement cards in exceptional cases.
• Cards in the Spell Deck or Pool Deck must not be distinguishable face down because of wear or damage.
• Cards with backs other than the standard Leros back require opaque sleeves with indistinguishable backs. This requirement applies to cards that can be part of the Spell Deck or Pool Deck.
• When sleeves are used, the same sleeves must be used for both decks.
# 2.2.2 Anatomy of a Bluthelden card
Cards have a front with game information and a back that generally has no game information. The front is the side with the play cost, name and card type. Double-sided Sovereigns are an exception: choose one side for the deck and keep that choice throughout the tournament.

Every card contains:
1. Its name, centred at the top of the front.
2. Its card type directly below the name, possibly including Mythic or Epic.
3. Rules text describing its effects and abilities. If no game-relevant text exists, italic flavour text appears instead.
4. Rarity at the bottom left: K, N, L or F.
5. Set and language information.
6. A copyright notice.
7. The artist's name.

Other information depends on card type (section 2.2.3). The background colour of the name and text boxes determines colour identity: White, Black, Orange, Green, Blue, Purple, Brown, Red or Grey (Equilibra).
# 2.2.3 Card types
A card's type appears below its name. A card with multiple types has all attributes of those types. If those types have different timing restrictions, use the timing that permits the most opportunities to play it.

Cards fall into two categories:
1. Permanents remain on the battlefield after being played. They require a slot or a defined position. If no such position is available, they cannot enter play and go to the Graveyard instead. Playing a permanent is called summoning (section 4.1).
2. Non-permanents go directly to the Graveyard after resolving. They do not require a battlefield slot. Playing a non-permanent is called casting (section 4.1).

A) Sovereigns
A Sovereign has life points at the top left and additional costs for other colours along the left edge of its artwork. Each deck must contain exactly one Sovereign, placed in the Sovereign slot in the Government Zone at the start of the game. It belongs to neither the Spell Deck nor the Pool Deck, but counts towards total deck size. Its colour limits the Pool Deck to cards of that colour and Grey (Equilibra) cards.

B) Runes
Runes have no additional icons and count as both Resource and Rune cards. Each deck contains exactly one Rune, placed in the Rune slot in the Government Zone at the start of the game. It belongs to neither deck but counts towards total deck size.

C) Resources
Resources also have Pool and Binding icons and a level. A player may put only one Resource into play per round, whether played from hand or found using the Rune's ability. Each Lane has one Resource slot in the Safety Zone. Only level 1 Resources may be put in an empty Resource slot.
A Resource above level 1 must be played on a Resource exactly one level lower. The lower-level Resource goes to the Graveyard as a result. Playing a Resource from hand is a special action and does not use the Reaction Chain (section 4.6).
A Resource opens its Lane for its player. Characters and permanents can be placed on that player's side of a Lane only if the Lane contains a Resource (section 2.3.1).

D) Characters
Characters have an ASP play cost, level icon, optional Binding and Pool icons, Resistance, Initiative and a POW/HP box. They may normally be placed only in unoccupied Character slots. A Character can be played from hand only if its level does not exceed the highest level among its controller's Resources in play. This also applies when the Rune puts a Character directly into play from the Pool Deck.
Regardless of its specific text, every Character can Move, Block and Attack when in an appropriate slot. A Character with an INI icon has one action per round. Printed RES, INI and POW/HP are base values (section 4.9).
Advisor is a special Character type with no INI or POW/HP values. An Advisor can be placed only in an Advisor slot. All other Character restrictions still apply.

E) Training
Trainings have an ASP cost, a Fundamental or Martial type in the name box, and optionally Spellpower. A Training can enter the battlefield only if it can attach to another permanent. Choose that permanent as a target when playing the Training (sections 4.1 and 6.1). The target must be in play both when the Training is played and when it resolves.
If the Training has Spellpower and its target has RES, compare them when the Training resolves or enters play. If Spellpower is at least RES, attach the Training. Otherwise it goes to the Graveyard without effect. Once successfully attached, it stays attached even if the trained card's RES later exceeds its Spellpower.
The attached permanent is trained; references such as “trained Character” refer to that permanent. If it leaves play, the Training goes to the Graveyard (section 4.8).

F) Artifacts
Artifacts have an ASP cost and may have Spellpower. They are placed in the permanent area, which can hold any number of permanents.

G) Rituals
Rituals have an ASP cost and may have Spellpower. They are permanents placed in the permanent area, which can hold any number of permanents.

H) Spells
Spells have an ASP cost and may have Spellpower and other subtypes. The types include Spell and Miracle; every Miracle also counts as a Spell. Spells are non-permanents and go to the Graveyard after resolving. Playing a Spell is called casting.
# 2.3 Battlefield and zones
The physical battlefield comprises all zones and positions of every player. Each player controls their own half and its slots. A card may be physically on the other side without changing control of that zone or slot.
Each player's battlefield is divided into Government Zone, Safety Zone and Combat Zone. These are the active zones, the battlefield in rules terms (section 4.8.1).
Four additional positions do not belong to a zone: Banished area, Graveyard, Pool Deck position and Spell Deck position. These are inactive areas (section 4.8.2).
The Reaction Chain, where played cards and effects are placed, and the hand are part of the game but not the battlefield. They are abstract game areas (section 4.8.3).
# 2.3.1 Lanes
The Safety and Combat Zones are divided into three vertical Lanes. Each Lane includes part of both players' Safety and Combat Zones. The Combat Zone positions within a Lane are called its War Zone.
# 2.3.2 Combat Zone and War Zones
The Combat Zone is between the players. It contains three War Zones, one per Lane. Each player has six Character positions in total, two per War Zone. A War Zone consists of both players' Front Rows in that Lane, above their Back Rows.
# 2.3.3 Safety Zones
Each player has a Safety Zone behind the Combat Zone, divided into three Lane sections. It can hold nine Characters and three Resources in total: three Characters and one Resource per Lane.
Each Lane's Safety Zone comprises a Back Row (two Character slots directly below the War Zone) and a Binding slot (one Character slot below the Back Row, left of the Resource slot).
# 2.3.4 Government Zone
Each player's Government Zone is below their Safety Zone. It contains a Sovereign slot, a Rune slot, an Advisor slot and any number of positions for permanents that cannot be placed in the Safety or Combat Zone.
# 2.3.5 Banished area
The Banished area holds cards placed there by a Banish effect.
# 2.3.6 Graveyard
The Graveyard holds cards that are destroyed or sacrificed, discarded from hand, cast and resolved as Spells, or placed there by a Gamestate Check (section 4.10).
# 2.4 Building a Bluthelden deck
The complete deck consists of the Spell Deck, Pool Deck, Sovereign and Rune. The Sovereign determines which Pool Deck cards are permitted and which cards are excluded from the deck.

Pool Deck
Only cards with a Pool icon may be included. They must match the Sovereign's colour or be Grey (Equilibra). Minimum and maximum sizes depend on the tournament format (chapter 11).

Spell Deck
Only cards with an ASP cost and not excluded by the Sovereign may be included. Size limits depend on the format (chapter 11).

Copy limits
The total number of same-name cards across both decks is limited by rarity:
• F — Fabulous: 1 copy.
• L — Legendary: 2 copies.
• N — Noted: 3 copies.
• K — Known: 4 copies.
Cards banned or restricted differently from their rarity are listed in the format's Banned & Restricted list.
# 3 Sequence of play
A round consists of the shared Start Phase, the Main Phase of the player with Initiative, the Main Phase of the player without Initiative, the Combat Phase and the End Phase. The following sections explain the steps, Reaction Chain and priority system.

At the start of the game, place the Sovereign and Rune in their slots. Both players draw six cards from their Spell Deck. Determine a winner by a random method such as a die roll or coin toss. That player chooses who initially receives Initiative. Mulligan decisions follow, then the first round begins at step 2, Draw Cards. Skip step 1, Beginning of the Round; no beginning-of-round effects trigger in the first round.

Mulligan
Once per player, set aside any number of cards from the starting hand face down, draw the same number from the Spell Deck, then shuffle the set-aside cards back into the Spell Deck. The player with Initiative makes their mulligan decision first, followed by the other player.
# 3.1 Round structure
# 3.1.1 Start Phase
The Start Phase has three steps:
1. Beginning of the round.
2. Draw cards.
3. Determine the player with Initiative (INI Check).
No player receives priority in any of these steps; neither hand cards nor abilities can be played.

Step 1: Unturn all cards first. Then put beginning-of-round triggers on the Reaction Chain and resolve them without allowing responses. If multiple effects trigger simultaneously, put the Initiative player's effects on first, then the other player's effects, and resolve them.

Step 2: Each player draws according to their hand size:
• Three or more cards in hand: draw one from either the Spell Deck or Pool Deck.
• Two or fewer cards: draw two from any combination of the Spell Deck and Pool Deck.
Players draw in Initiative order and announce which deck they are drawing from. Once both have drawn, proceed to step 3.

Step 3: Identify the Character with the highest INI. Its controller receives Initiative. On a tie, the previous Initiative player keeps it. If no Character has INI greater than 0, treat this as a tie and apply the tie rule (section 3.1.4). Proceed to Main Phase 1.
# 3.1.2 Main Phase 1: player with Initiative
This is the turn of the player with Initiative. Put “at the beginning of your turn” abilities on the Reaction Chain first, followed by “at the beginning of your opponent's turn” abilities.
The Initiative player may play any type of card from hand and activate abilities in any order. They may also use the special actions Play a Resource and Move (section 4.6).
The other player may activate abilities, but may not play cards from hand or use those two special actions.
When both players pass with an empty Reaction Chain, proceed to Main Phase 2.
# 3.1.3 Main Phase 2: player without Initiative
This is the turn of the player without Initiative. Put “at the beginning of your turn” abilities on the Reaction Chain first, followed by “at the beginning of your opponent's turn” abilities.
The player without Initiative may play any type of card from hand and activate abilities in any order. They may also use Play a Resource and Move (section 4.6).
The Initiative player may activate abilities, but may not play cards from hand or use those two special actions.
When both pass with an empty Reaction Chain, perform an INI Check and proceed to the Combat Phase.
# 3.1.4 Combat Phase
Neither player may play cards from hand during this phase. Each Character in play has one action per round. If not already spent, it may use that action to Attack or Move as the acting Character, or to Block in reaction as a non-acting Character. Each of these uses an action for the round.

Step 1 — Determine the acting Character and active Lane
Find the highest-INI Character in an unresolved Lane that has at least one action remaining, has not yet been the acting Character and has INI greater than 0. It becomes the acting Character and its Lane becomes the active Lane.

Step 2 — Choose an action
The acting Character's controller receives priority. After both players pass, choose:
a) Attack: consumes an action.
b) Move: consumes an action.
c) Do nothing: does not consume an action.

An Attack requires the acting Character to be in the active Lane's War Zone, unturned, with an action remaining and INI greater than 0, and not to have entered the battlefield during this Combat Phase.

Resolving an attack
Turn the attacking Character; it is now attacking. Attacks normally attack the opposing Sovereign. The acting Character's controller receives priority before blockers are chosen. When both players pass, the defending player may choose one blocker for each attack action. Turn each blocker and spend its action; that attack is blocked.
The acting Character's controller receives priority again. When both pass, combat damage is dealt: attacker and blocker simultaneously deal damage equal to their POW to each other, provided both are still in the same War Zone. An unblocked attacker deals its damage to the defending Sovereign. The acting Character's controller receives priority again; when both pass, proceed to step 3.

Blocking requires a Character in the same War Zone as the attacker, unturned, with at least one action remaining for this combat. Blocking is voluntary. Effects that prohibit blocking prevent only the choice of this reaction; they do not stop another effect from making an object a blocking defender.

Step 3 — Find the next acting Character
In the active Lane, find the highest-INI Character with an action remaining that is unturned, has not yet been the acting Character and has INI greater than 0. It becomes the acting Character; repeat step 2. Continue until no Character in the Lane can act, then mark the Lane resolved and return to step 1.
Continue until all Lanes are resolved. A Character placed in an already resolved Lane does not reopen it and cannot become the acting Character.

Initiative ties
If eligible Characters controlled by different players tie, the Character controlled by the player holding Initiative from the last INI Check acts first. If tied Characters share a controller, that player chooses which acts first.

Step 4 — End of combat
Once every Lane is resolved, trigger all end-of-combat abilities. Characters in a War Zone at the end of combat that performed a combat action (Attack or Block) count as having survived until the end of the round. The Initiative player receives priority. When both pass, perform an INI Check for the end of the round: the controller of the highest-INI Character receives Initiative. Proceed to the End Phase.
# 3.1.5 End Phase
Both players may play Spells and Trainings from hand at the end of the round. The phase has three steps.

Step 1 — At the end of the round
Trigger end-of-round abilities. Put the Initiative player's abilities on the Reaction Chain first, then the other player's. The Initiative player receives priority. When both pass with an empty chain, proceed to step 2.

Step 2 — Discard
Starting with the Initiative player, both players discard to the maximum hand size of eight. Resolve resulting triggers and repeat step 2. For simultaneous triggers, the Initiative player's go on the chain first. No player receives priority. Then proceed to step 3.

Step 3 — Round transition
All “until end of round” effects end. No player receives priority. If Characters die or abilities trigger as a result, resolve those effects and repeat step 2. Order simultaneous triggers by putting the Initiative player's effects on first. Finally, all remaining ASP expire. Proceed to the next round's Start Phase.
# 3.2 Priority and the Reaction Chain
The Reaction Chain establishes a clear order for resolving cards, abilities and their effects. The priority system determines who may actively add something to it.
# 3.2.1 Priority
The player with priority may play cards or activate abilities and keeps priority until they choose to pass. When both players pass consecutively, resolve the most recently added card or ability. Priority then returns to the player who first receives it in the current phase:
• Main Phase 1: the player with Initiative.
• Main Phase 2: the player without Initiative.
• Combat Phase: the acting Character's controller.
• End Phase: the player with Initiative.
If both pass with an empty chain, advance to the next step.

The chain resolves last in, first out. It is part of the game, not the battlefield (section 2.3). Played cards move from their previous location, such as the hand, onto the chain, then to their destination after resolving. They are no longer in their previous location once played.
Activated and triggered abilities on the chain are independent of their source. They remain and resolve even if that source leaves play or changes zones.
If an effect affects cards without “target”, without instructing a player to “choose”, and without a deterministic identification rule, its controller determines the affected cards when applying the effect. This is not targeting.
# 4 Game actions and card rules
Playing cards and activating abilities require priority. Cards from hand must also obey their card type's timing restrictions (section 3.1):
• Characters, Artifacts, Rituals: only during your own turn.
• Trainings and Spells: during your own turn and at the end of the round.
• Resources: only as a special action during your own turn with an empty Reaction Chain.
Activated abilities may have restrictions stated in their card text. Otherwise they have no additional timing restrictions.
# 4.1 Playing a card
Playing a card usually requires paying its printed ASP cost at the top left, and may require additional costs. If timing and priority permit playing it, use this order:
1. Announce the card from hand.
2. Choose a face-down mode if available, such as Hideout or another face-down method.
3. Put the card in the next unoccupied position on the Reaction Chain, face down if such a mode was chosen, otherwise face up. It is now an object on the chain, no longer a hand card.
4. Choose other modes, including Rise and any required effect modes.
5. Determine all targets: the destination slot for a permanent and all required “choose”/“target” choices.
6. Determine total costs. Apply, in order: ASP increases (including the Sovereign's off-colour surcharge); ASP reductions; effects setting minimum ASP costs; effects setting total ASP costs; additional-cost increases; additional-cost reductions; effects setting minimum additional costs; effects setting additional costs.
7. Pay total costs: deduct ASP from the reserve, then pay non-ASP additional costs such as life, discards or sacrifices.

Abilities triggered during this process wait until the card has been fully played. Then place the active player's triggers on the chain, followed by the inactive player's. The player who played the card retains priority.
An incorrectly played card, for example with an illegal target or insufficient resources, is an illegal action. Abort and reverse the process (chapter 14).
# 4.2 Activating and triggering abilities
Abilities enter the Reaction Chain either through activation, with activation costs paid, or automatically when a triggering event occurs.
For both types, determine and lock in all relevant values, decisions and references when the ability is activated or triggers. Later changes to the source or reference values do not affect resolution unless the text explicitly says otherwise, such as “as this resolves”.

Activated abilities
These always have the form “[cost]: effect”. Wording such as “activate only if…”, “activate only once per round…” or “activate only as long as…” adds activation requirements. If a requirement is not met, activation is not allowed.
The player must have priority and control the card. Then:
1. Announce the ability.
2. Put it in the next unoccupied chain position, fixing its position.
3. Choose any required effect modes.
4. Announce all required “choose”/“target” choices.
5. Determine total costs in this order: ASP increases; ASP reductions; minimum ASP costs; total ASP costs; additional-cost increases; additional-cost reductions; minimum additional costs; fixed additional costs.
6. Pay ASP costs from the reserve, then non-ASP costs such as turning, life, discards or sacrifices.
Triggers caused during activation wait until it is complete, then go on the chain in active-player, inactive-player order. The activating player keeps priority.
Illegal activation, including an illegal target, insufficient resources or illegal timing, is aborted and reversed (chapter 14).

Triggered abilities
They trigger automatically when their event occurs, signalled by wording such as “when”, “whenever” or “at the beginning/end of…”. Triggering does not require priority.
Put triggered abilities on the chain once their triggering event is fully complete. For simultaneous triggers, first place the abilities of the player who first receives priority in the current phase, then the other player's. Each player orders their own simultaneous abilities.

Delayed triggered abilities
Effects may create abilities that wait for a specified future event. Until triggering, they have no independent card or zone relationship. Define their specified values, conditions and references when they are created. When their event occurs, put them on the chain and resolve them like other triggered abilities.

Targets and modes
The controller of the source chooses required targets and modes when putting a trigger on the chain. All targets must be legal then. After all triggers have been placed, assign priority according to the phase (section 3.1) and resolve abilities under the normal chain and resolution rules (section 4.3).
# 4.3 Resolving a card or ability
When both players pass consecutively, resolve the top card or ability on the chain, last in, first out. Follow its rules text in order.
A permanent moves to its designated battlefield position if available; otherwise it goes to the Graveyard. A Spell goes to the Graveyard. An ability does not change zones; it ceases to exist after resolving. Then assign priority under section 3.2.1.
If all of a card or ability's selected targets have become illegal, for example through a zone change, it has no effect. An ability then ceases to exist; a card moves from the chain to the Graveyard.
# 4.3.1 Spellpower versus Resistance: RES Check
If an effect from a source with Spellpower would affect a card with RES, perform a RES Check for each affected target at resolution.
If the source's Spellpower is at least the target's RES, the effect applies normally to that target. If RES exceeds Spellpower, the effect does not affect that target. Other targets and other parts of the effect remain unaffected unless they also require a RES Check.
# 4.4 Astral points
Astral points (ASP) pay for effects and cards. Generated ASP are collected in the ASP reserve, an abstract area rather than a battlefield zone.
ASP are usually generated by abilities. These use the Reaction Chain, are not immediate, and can be responded to. Playing a card or activating an ability is a closed sequence (sections 4.1 and 4.2), so all required ASP must be generated and already in the reserve before starting.
ASP remain until the end of the round. All remaining ASP expire in step 3 of the End Phase (section 3.1.5).
# 4.5 Additional costs
Additional costs are paid as part of playing a card or activating an effect. Like ASP payment, paying them does not use the Reaction Chain. Pay all additional costs in the order required by the card or effect.
The Sovereign's off-colour surcharge is an additional cost applied in step 6a of section 4.1.

Common additional costs:
• Sacrifice a permanent: only its controller may sacrifice it; move it from play to the Graveyard.
• Discard cards: discard only cards in your own hand, moving them to the Graveyard.
• Remove counters: remove the exact named counter type. Unless stated otherwise, a permanent referring to counters as a cost means counters on itself. For example, “Remove 1 Time Counter: Draw a card” on an Artifact cannot use another permanent's Time Counter.
• Pay life: subtract the amount directly from the Sovereign's current life points. This is not damage. The player must have enough life to pay the full cost.
• Turn: rotate an unturned card by 180 degrees. An already turned card cannot pay this cost.
• Change zones: for example, return a permanent to hand or move a card from the Graveyard to the Banished area.
# 4.6 Special actions
Special actions do not use the Reaction Chain. They happen immediately and cannot be interrupted or prevented by an opponent's response. They require priority.

1. Play a Resource: put a Resource from hand into an empty Resource slot, or replace one when levelling up. This is allowed only if that player has not already placed a Resource in a Resource slot this round, during their own Main Phase and with an empty chain.
2. Move: turn a Character and move it to its designated new slot, for example from the Safety Zone to the War Zone. The Character must be unturned with INI greater than 0. Move uses an action for the round. During combat, only the acting Character may do this, and it must have an action remaining.
3. Turn a face-down card face up: when a card's ability permits this by paying a cost, for example Hideout, it is a special action.

If an effect instructs a special action, all its restrictions, including timing and action use, still apply unless the effect states otherwise.
Example: “Move It!” says “Move target Character”. At resolution, that Character performs Move. The Spell's controller controls the movement and makes all its decisions. The Character turns, spends an action and may move only to a free slot in an adjacent row (War Zone, Back Row or Binding slot).
# 4.7 Characteristic determination system (EBS)
Every card and game object has defined characteristics at all times. Determine them by applying characteristic layers 1–7 strictly in order.

Layer 1 — Owner, identity and base characteristics
The owner is the player whose deck contained the physical card at game start. A token's owner is the player under whose control it was created. Ownership never changes.
Start from the printed card or the token's creation definition. Apply copy effects here, replacing the printed information with that of the copied object. This establishes name, ASP and additional-cost symbols, main type (such as Character, Resource or Spell), supertype (such as Mythic, Epic, Fundamental, Martial or Advisor), subtype (such as Human/Undead), colour identity, rules text, and printed POW, HP, RES, INI and Spellpower.

Layer 2 — Control
By default, the controller is the player who put the card into play. Apply control-changing effects here.

Layer 3 — Text and name
Start with layer 1's text and name, then apply effects changing them.

Layer 4 — Type and colour
Start with layer 1's types and colours, then apply effects changing or adding them.

Layer 5 — Abilities
Start with the abilities in the rules text after layer 3. Apply ability-granting and ability-removing effects here.

Layer 6 — Setting base values
Start with the printed numerical values from layer 1. Effects setting POW/HP, RES, INI or Spellpower to a specific value overwrite that value as the base for the next layer.

Layer 7 — Modified values
Apply changes to the layer 6 base in this order:
1. Multipliers.
2. Additive modifiers.
3. Subtractive modifiers.
4. Exchanges of values, such as swapping POW and RES.

Timestamp order
If two effects conflict within one layer, the newer timestamp wins: the effect that entered play or was activated later.

Restrictions on actions
For conflicting permissions and prohibitions on actions such as playing, activating, attacking or moving, “can't” overrides “can”. For example, a Character required to attack each combat if able cannot attack if a Training says it cannot attack.
# 4.8 Zones and object identity
This chapter defines card status by location under chapter 2 and the mechanical consequences of changing location.
# 4.8.1 Active zones: the battlefield
Only cards physically in the Combat, Safety or Government Zone are in play. These cards are permanents.
Within active zones, a card remains the same object. Its turned state, counters and attached Trainings remain. Moving between active zones, for example from Safety Zone to Combat Zone, is not a zone change for object identity and does not trigger enters-play or leaves-play effects.
# 4.8.2 Inactive areas
The Graveyard, Banished area and deck positions are not in play. Cards there are inactive objects and have no modified values. When an effect asks for a value for a search or reference, use the printed value.
# 4.8.3 Abstract areas
These are neither zones nor physical battlefield positions:
1. Hand: cards here are hidden information to the opponent.
2. Reaction Chain: a temporary area for played cards and effects.
3. ASP reserve: an abstract counter for available astral points.
# 4.8.4 Zone changes and resetting
Moving between zone categories, such as from an active zone to an inactive area or from hand into play, makes a card a new object.
• Reset: the new object has no connection to the previous one. Remove all counters and damage counters; temporary and static effects end. Existing or future references to the old object become invalid unless the zone-changing effect explicitly provides otherwise.
• Tokens: tokens exist only in active zones. As soon as a token leaves an active zone, it ceases to exist as an automatic Gamestate Check.
• Trainings: when an attached card changes zones, its Training stays behind unattached. It does not follow and goes to the Graveyard through a Gamestate Check (section 4.10).
# 4.9 Definitions of values
Bluthelden® distinguishes base values, modified values and current values to resolve situations unambiguously.
# 4.9.1 Base value
The base value is the starting point for calculation: printed POW, HP, RES, INI, Spellpower or cost. Outside play, including Graveyard, hand, deck and Banished area, only base values exist; ignore modifications.
An effect that sets a value, such as “becomes 3/3”, temporarily sets a new base for the object in play (layer 6 in section 4.7).
# 4.9.2 Modified value
The modified value is the numerical maximum a Character can reach from its values and modifiers. Apply all permanent and temporary bonuses, penalties and multipliers through EBS layers 1–7. It caps healing (Regeneration) and is the basis for the current value.
# 4.9.3 Current value
The current value describes the actual state of an object in play. Card or effect references to a number, such as “destroy a Character with HP 2 or less”, use the current value.

Current HP = modified HP − accumulated damage. At 0 or less, the Character is destroyed by a Gamestate Check (section 4.10).
Current life points = modified life points − accumulated damage.
Current POW, RES and INI normally equal their modified values.

Exception: turned RES and INI
• System checks: when checking action requirements (Attack, Block, Move) or automatic comparisons (INI Check, Spellpower/RES Check such as attaching Training), current INI and RES are 0.
• Card references: when a card or ability asks for a value, including target selection or exchanging RES and POW, the current value remains the modified value.

For queries, treat a negative current value as 0. Modifier calculations still use the actual value. Example: RES 1 with two RES−1 counters is −1, but all RES comparisons treat it as 0.
# 4.10 Gamestate Check (GSC)
A GSC automatically corrects illegal game states. It does not use the Reaction Chain and cannot be interrupted.

Perform a GSC after resolving an effect, immediately before a player would receive priority, and between First Damage and normal combat damage.

Check and correct the following in order. Repeat the GSC if corrections create new states, for example death triggers, before granting priority:
1. Sovereign at 0 or less life: its controller loses. If both are affected simultaneously, the game is a draw.
2. Empty-deck draw: a player required to draw from an empty Pool or Spell Deck loses. If both are affected simultaneously, the game is a draw.
3. Lethal damage: destroy Characters with current HP of 0 or less and put them in the Graveyard.
4. Tokens outside active zones, for example in hand or Graveyard, cease to exist.
5. Epic: if a player controls multiple same-name Epic permanents in one Lane, they choose one and sacrifice the others.
6. Mythic: if a player controls multiple same-name Mythic permanents anywhere on the battlefield, they choose one and sacrifice the others.
7. Unattached Training: destroy a Training in play that is not attached to another permanent and put it in the Graveyard.
8. No slot: a permanent that cannot be legally placed because no free slot is available goes to its owner's Graveyard instead.
# 5 Special combat mechanics
The structure of combat, Initiative, basic attacking and blocking are in section 3.1.4. Special actions, particularly Move, are in section 4.6. This chapter covers modifications to normal combat.
# 5.1 Attack variants
1. Ranged Combat: from the Back Row, a Character with this ability may attack a Character in the same Lane's War Zone. This counts as an Attack combat action and uses one action for the round. It does not cause a Block reaction.

2. Targeted Strike: when declaring an attack, choose the opposing Sovereign or a Character in the opposing War Zone of the same Lane. A chosen Character is forced to block even without a remaining Block action. If available, consume its action and turn it as for a normal block. This modifies attack declaration; it is not a triggered ability and is not prevented by targeting protection such as Untargetable or Spectral Ward.

3. Two-Handed Combat: the Character has two actions per round instead of one. When choosing Attack, declare one or two attacks. Two attacks consume both available actions. The opponent may declare a separate Block reaction for each, if they have enough eligible Characters. Damage from all those attack actions is assigned simultaneously.
Example: a 3/3 Character declares two attacks. The opponent has one eligible 2/2 blocker, which blocks the first attack. The blocker takes 3 damage, the opposing Sovereign takes 3 from the unblocked attack, and the attacker takes 2 from the blocker.
# 5.2 Block variants
Normal blockers must be in the same War Zone, unturned and have an action remaining (section 3.1.4). These abilities modify that:
1. Flying: only Characters with Flying or Skyguard may block a Flying attacker.
2. Unblockable: the Character cannot be blocked; its attack does not allow a legal Block reaction.
3. Barrier: it takes no slot but belongs to its War Zone. If it is its player's only Character in that War Zone, all attacking Characters gain Targeted Strike with the Barrier automatically chosen as the attacked Character.
# 5.3 Advanced damage resolution
Damage is normally simultaneous. These abilities modify it:

First Damage
Add a First Damage segment in which only Characters with First Damage deal damage. Perform a GSC before the normal segment (section 4.10). In the normal segment, surviving Characters that did not deal First Damage deal their damage.

Punch
If combat damage to a blocker exceeds that blocker's current HP, deal the excess to the opposing Sovereign instead. Attacker and blocker must be in the same War Zone at damage resolution. If the blocker was removed beforehand, use 0 for its current HP.
Damage to Sovereign = attacker's POW − blocker's current HP.
# 6 Rules terminology
Appendix I lists terms, Appendix II conditions and Appendix III ability and keyword wording. The following explanations apply independently of individual abilities, keywords and conditions.
# 6.1 Determining affected cards
When an effect refers to cards without “target” or “choose”, and no deterministic rule identifies them, its controller determines the affected cards when applying the effect.
This is not targeting, does not trigger abilities triggered by targeting, and is not affected by selection-restricting effects.
Example: a Spell with Spellpower 4 says “Return a Character to its owner's hand”. The opponent controls two Untargetable Characters, A with RES 3 and B with RES 5. Either can be selected despite Untargetable, but a RES Check still applies. A returns to its owner's hand; choosing B produces no effect because RES 5 exceeds Spellpower 4.
# 6.2 Conditions
Conditions are binary keyword mechanics created by effects. A card either has a condition or does not; it cannot have multiple instances of the same condition. Condition counters or keyword abilities may grant them, but multiple sources still create only one instance of the condition.
Binary states created solely by the game rules, rather than granted by cards or effects, are not conditions in this sense, particularly turned and bound. See Appendix II for the list.
# 7 Tournament operation
# 7.1 Purpose and scope
This rulebook governs Bluthelden® tournaments and supplements the game rules with organisational, formal and procedural provisions for competitive play. It aims to ensure a fair, consistent and understandable tournament process, comparable conditions for all participants, and clearly defined violations and consequences.
It applies to sanctioned events explicitly designated as such by tournament management. For unsanctioned games, demonstrations or private events, it applies only as agreed by organisers or players.
It does not replace the game rules. Game mechanics, card effects, timing and definitions follow the game rules and binding card text. House rules, alternative formats and simplified rules are not permitted in sanctioned tournaments.
# 7.2 Binding rules and hierarchy
Apply the following order:
1. Card text: rules text published in the official Bluthelden spoiler is binding and overrides general descriptions and differing printed text.
2. Game rules: govern mechanics, definitions, timing and interactions.
3. Tournament rules: govern organisation, procedures, responsibilities, violations and their treatment.
4. Tournament management and Judge decisions: binding during the tournament to interpret and enforce existing rules and preserve integrity.
If interpretations conflict during an event, tournament management's decision governs that event, even if later corrected or clarified.
# 7.3 Rule Enforcement Level (REL)
REL describes the intended strictness, organisation and enforcement. It affects expected rules knowledge, treatment of violations, the type and severity of measures and penalties, and interpretation of corrections.
Management announces the level and its application before the tournament. Lower levels prioritise smooth play and the player experience; higher levels prioritise compliance, competitive integrity and consistent enforcement. Deliberate violations, deception and unsporting conduct are unacceptable at every level.

The four levels are:
• REL 0 — Casual Play (unsanctioned).
• REL 1 — Basic Play.
• REL 2 — Advanced Play.
• REL 3 — Competitive Play.
# 7.4 Tournament software and result reporting
Sanctioned Bluthelden® events must be run and evaluated using the official tournament software. This is a condition of sanctioning.
The software manages players, rounds and pairings, records and evaluates match results, documents the event consistently, and handles official reporting and archiving.
An event not run or correctly reported through that software is unsanctioned and counts as Casual Play, regardless of how it is described publicly.
Management is responsible for correct use and complete, accurate reporting. Players must cooperate, particularly in confirming results and correctly identifying matches. Information, versions and access conditions are available at www.bluthelden.net.
# 8 Tournament organisation and roles
# 8.1 Tournament management
Management is organisationally responsible for compliance, scheduling, communication, fairness and tournament integrity. It must establish and announce all relevant conditions so that participants compete on equal and understandable terms, including format, REL, match structure, time rules, decklists, materials and result reporting.
# 8.1.1 Defining and announcing the event framework
Before the event, management must announce at least:
• Format and variant, for example Constructed or Limited.
• Match structure.
• Time rules, particularly round length.
• REL and its implications for strictness and error treatment.
• Reporting procedures and, where applicable, number of rounds, cut rules and final-round structure.
Changes after the start are allowed only if essential, not disproportionately disruptive and equally applicable to all players. Announce them immediately.
# 8.1.2 Tournament flow and time management
Management organises pairings, round starts and ends, and result recording. It ensures the announced time conditions can be met and minimises organisational delays.
It may make appropriate arrangements, including seating, assigning match areas, breaks, and granting or refusing time extensions under the time rules.
# 8.1.3 Decision-making and enforcement
Management enforces these rules and may appoint Judges and delegate duties. It coordinates Judges and ensures consistent decisions and measures. With several Judges, a Head Judge may assume coordination and tournament leadership and is the final authority on rules interpretation and application.
Management decides organisational disputes. Rules questions and violations follow the hierarchy in section 7.2. Management's decisions remain binding for the current event to ensure consistent completion.
# 8.2 Judges
Judges are appointed to rule on questions, monitor compliance and apply measures under this rulebook. They protect integrity and smooth tournament operation.
They act for management within their responsibilities. Their decisions bind the current match and event unless replaced or overturned through a valid appeal.
# 8.2.1 Responsibility within a match
A Judge intervenes when a player asks a rules question, the game state is unclear, a violation is suspected or match-related tournament operation is disrupted. The Judge establishes the facts, rules, and directs continuation.
The Judge may pause a match as necessary to rule, clarify the state or apply a measure. Keep interruptions as short as necessary.
# 8.2.2 Rules interpretation and procedural decisions
Judges use card text and game rules, applying tournament provisions with regard to their purpose, particularly fairness, consistency and clarity.
If facts cannot be fully established, the Judge decides to the best of their knowledge on the available information. Players must cooperate and answer truthfully.
Judges must not reveal or imply hidden information. Questions must establish facts without giving improper clues.
# 8.2.3 Handling violations
Classify a detected or reported violation under chapter 12, apply measures under chapter 13 and, where necessary and allowed, corrections under chapter 14.
Document incidents when required by severity, repetition, escalation risk or REL. Records should allow later review and help management apply consistent standards.
# 8.2.4 Events without Judges
If no Judges are appointed, management assumes all Judge responsibilities and makes the rules and procedural decisions assigned to Judges by this rulebook.
# 8.3 Player responsibilities
Players must contribute to integrity through conduct and play. They are responsible for their materials, legal actions and communication that permits the opponent to interact legally.
Deliberate violations, deception and procedural abuse are prohibited. Respectful, fair conduct is expected at every REL.
# 8.3.1 Rules knowledge and cooperation
Players must know the applicable rules sufficiently to participate properly. Ignorance does not excuse illegal play.
They must actively assist with rules questions, game-state clarification and investigations, and answer management and Judges truthfully. Deliberately withholding relevant information, misleading officials or giving contradictory accounts may be a serious violation.
# 8.3.2 Clear game state
Keep cards in the correct areas, zones identifiable, and counters, conditions and aids understandable. Clarify uncertainty promptly. If players disagree or cannot clarify the state, immediately call a Judge or management. Do not continue from a disputed state if that could create an advantage.
# 8.3.3 Communication and understandable actions
Perform actions so the opponent can recognise, understand and, where permitted, respond to them. This particularly applies to timing, priority and response windows.
Do not compress, rush or conceal actions so that a legal response becomes practically impossible. Provide clear announcements and recognisable pauses where required for proper interaction.
# 8.3.4 Play aids: markers, tokens and notes
Aids are allowed if they clearly represent the game state without confusion. Explain their meaning to the opponent on request.
Do not use them to mark or infer hidden information. Different sleeves, intentional wear or recognisable backs revealing hidden information are prohibited.
Unless mandatory rules state otherwise, management announces whether and to what extent notes are permitted.
# 8.3.5 Protecting hidden information and handling materials
Manage hidden information so the opponent cannot gain improper access. Hold and manage hand cards and other hidden areas without accidentally revealing card identities or quantities.
Use only legal materials and maintain them without marks or identifying features. Report problems arising during the event, such as damaged sleeves, to a Judge or management immediately.
# 8.3.6 Calling officials and preventing abuse
Promptly call management or a Judge for rules questions, uncertainty or suspected violations. Calls must not be used as pressure or deliberate delay.
Do not wait intentionally to gain more advantage from an error later. Raise an identified error promptly; the official decides how to handle it.
# 9 Tournament materials and preparation
# 9.1 Permitted materials
Only approved materials are allowed in sanctioned events. Each player is responsible for ensuring their materials comply and create no improper advantage.
Only original Bluthelden® cards are permitted. Proxies, home-printed or handwritten replacements and digital representations of physical cards are prohibited unless explicitly approved by management, for example temporary replacements it issues for damaged cards.
Sleeves are strongly recommended but not mandatory if cards have no marks, wear or distinguishing features. Face-down cards must not reveal identity or affiliation through wear, markings, deformation or other features.
# 9.1.1 Sleeves
When used:
• All cards in both the Spell Deck and Pool Deck must use identical sleeves.
• Sleeves must not identify cards through marks, writing, symbols or wear.
• Transparent sleeves are allowed only if card backs are completely identical and reveal neither identity nor card type.
Replace damaged or conspicuous sleeves immediately when instructed by management or a Judge.
# 9.1.2 Tokens, counters and other aids
Aids are allowed if they represent the state unambiguously and their meaning is clear to both players at all times.
They must not represent or replace hidden information or allow hidden cards or states to be inferred. Management may prohibit aids that cause confusion or disrupt the event.
# 9.2 Deck construction and legality
Game rules establish deck construction; these tournament rules add requirements for sanctioned play. A legal deck must:
• Satisfy game-rule construction requirements.
• Contain only approved cards.
• Follow the event's official Banned & Restricted list.
• Remain unchanged throughout the event unless these rules or management explicitly permit a change.
The applicable list is bound to the event and part of its official announcement. Deviations are prohibited. Events deliberately departing from it are unsanctioned Casual Play, regardless of being described as tournaments.
Management must announce the list clearly before the event or provide an unambiguous official source reference.
# 9.3 Decklists
Players must submit a decklist before the event when required by management or this rulebook. It verifies deck legality and consistent competitive conditions.
# 9.3.1 Decklist requirements
A list must be complete, unambiguous and legible, containing at least:
• Player name.
• Player name as recorded in the tournament software.
• Every card and its quantity.
• Any format-specific additional information, such as a separate Sealed pool list.
Incomplete, illegible or contradictory lists are erroneous.
# 9.3.2 Submission and corrections
Submit at the time set by management. Lists generally cannot change after the tournament starts.
Management may permit correction of errors found before the start. Errors found afterwards are violations handled under chapters 12 and 13.
# 9.4 Deck checks
Management and appointed Judges may check consistency between deck and list and legality of materials. Checks may be random, scheduled at particular stages or based on specific suspicion.
# 9.4.1 Scope of deck checks
Checks may cover card count, agreement with the submitted list, rarity and format restrictions and other construction rules, and marked or worn sleeves and cards.
Minimise disruption; resulting delays may be accounted for under the time rules.
# 9.4.2 Discrepancies and consequences
Management or the Judge classifies discrepancies and decides the next steps under chapters 12 and 13 and the event's REL.
They may order a deck to be made legal. Correction does not exempt its player from measures or penalties.
# 10 Match and round structure
# 10.1 Definitions
A game is one self-contained Bluthelden® game started, played and ended under the game rules.
A match is the smallest unit relevant to tournament scoring and normally consists of exactly one game.
A round is a tournament stage containing parallel matches. It ends when all its matches finish or are decided under the time rules.
# 10.2 Match formats
Bluthelden® is designed for Best-of-One (BO1): one game determines the match. Deviations require explicit provision and organisational support by management.
As an optional exception, management may use Best-of-Three (BO3) for clearly defined stages. A match has up to three games and is won upon winning two.
BO3 takes substantially more time. Use it only where no fixed time limit is needed or enough time is assured for all possible games. Suitable examples include post-cut finals (Top 4/Top 8), single-elimination events, invitation-only events or stages with particularly high competitive requirements. BO3 is not recommended for ordinary rounds with strict time limits.
# 10.3 Preparation and starting player
Randomise both decks by shuffling before play; this should take no more than three minutes. Offer each to the opponent for one cut. If they cut, exchange the former top and bottom portions.
Start according to the game rules. In multi-game matches such as BO3, the loser of the previous game determines the starting player for subsequent games instead of random selection. After a draw, the previous starting player remains unchanged.
# 10.4 Time rules
Management announces round length before the event. Choose enough time for a match at the intended REL to finish normally. A round should generally not be shorter than 50 minutes.
Timing starts at the official round start and ends at management's announced time expiry.
# 10.4.1 Time extensions
Management or a Judge may grant an extension for delays outside the players' control, particularly Judge calls, deck checks or organisational interruptions. Extensions compensate for such delays and must be limited to what is necessary.
# 10.4.2 End-of-time procedure
Handle unfinished matches according to the procedure management announced before the event.
If no result is reached in the allotted time, grant two additional game rounds, beginning after the current game round. If there is still no winner, the game and normally the match are drawn. Both players must continue promptly without unnecessary delays.
BO3 should generally have no time limit unless a specific tournament mode explicitly provides otherwise. If time expires with no match winner, the player with more game wins wins the match. Equal wins result in a draw.
# 11 Tournament formats
Formats define how decks are built, which cards are allowed and the event's structural conditions. Game rules provide the basic deckbuilding and game rules; this chapter adds only tournament-format provisions.
Management must clearly define and announce the format before the event.
# 11.1 Constructed
Players use decks built in advance, complying with game rules and the event's restrictions.
# 11.1.1 General principles
• Decks must be complete before the event begins.
• No deck changes after the event starts or the decklist is submitted.
• Only the event's officially designated Banned & Restricted list applies (section 9.2).
# 11.1.2 Traditional Bluthelden Constructed
This is the primary intended Constructed format for sanctioned events. Cards from all released sets and expansions, and approved promotional cards, may be used. Apply all normal construction rules with no additional restrictions apart from the current Banned & Restricted list.
Total deck size must be 60–80 cards, including Sovereign and Rune. The Pool Deck must contain 18–20 cards. The Spell Deck must contain at least 40 cards.
# 11.2 Limited
Decks are built wholly or partly during the event from an assigned pool. Management announces the variant and card distribution before the start.
# 11.2.1 General principles
• Only cards from the assigned pool may be used.
• Build within the time set by management.
• Once construction ends, the same deck consistency and legality requirements as Constructed apply, except for modified deck sizes.
# 11.2.2 Starter Limited
Players use designated starter products. Management determines the products and whether, and to what extent, changes are allowed, for example using additional boosters.
# 11.2.3 Sealed
Use a fixed number of boosters plus half an Event Booster. Choose the Sovereign and matching Rune after opening the sealed boosters, during construction time. Available choices follow the Sovereigns and Runes in Event Boosters; those opened in ordinary boosters may also be used. No changes after construction time.
The total deck contains at least 50 cards, including Sovereign and Rune. The Pool Deck has 9–18 cards; the remainder form the Spell Deck. All other construction rules remain in effect.
# 11.2.4 Draft
Open sealed Bluthelden boosters in a set order and draft cards into a construction pool. Each player also receives half an Event Booster.
The total deck contains at least 40 cards, including Sovereign and Rune. The Pool Deck has 9–18 cards; the remainder form the Spell Deck. Other construction rules depend on the draft variant, which management must announce in advance.
# 11.3 Format compliance and sanctioning
The chosen format is a binding part of sanctioning. An event is sanctioned only when it uses an approved format, has no unauthorised deviations, and is run and reported under section 7.4.
Deliberate format deviations or unauthorised special rules make the event unsanctioned Casual Play.
# 12 Violations and infraction categories
Complex game states can cause mistakes even with careful play. These rules aim for fair, consistent treatment resistant to manipulation, rather than harshly punishing every error.
This chapter defines violations and classifications. Chapter 13 covers measures and penalties; chapter 14 covers corrections. What matters is whether an incident threatens equal competition or creates cheating potential, not merely whether some advantage is theoretically possible. Apply measures according to REL.
# 12.1 Violations and infraction categories
Violations are events or actions departing from card text, game rules or tournament rules that may impair legal tournament operation or match integrity.
This chapter defines classification principles and infractions; chapters 13 and 14 govern measures, penalties and corrections.
# 12.2 General principles
A game should be decided fairly, not through accidental or deliberate disregard of rules. Tournament operation must also remain practical. Not every error can be completely reversed without creating new inequality. Integrity and fairness take precedence over perfect reconstruction.
Handling violations protects integrity and equal treatment, establishes or preserves a clear state, prevents planned or repeated misuse, and supports acceptance through consistency and transparency.
# 12.2.1 Responsibility and cooperation
Each player is responsible for legal actions, proper materials, a clear board state, and time and communication duties.
Both share responsibility for promptly raising uncertainty or suspected violations. Do not wait to gain an advantage from later intervention.
Immediately call a Judge or management when a violation is noticed or suspected. Do not continue speculatively if this could complicate clarification or increase the advantage from the error.
# 12.3 Intent, advantage and classification
Distinguish unintentional errors without deceptive intent from deliberate or knowingly accepted violations.
A violation may be serious without proven intent if repeated, significantly affecting the match or objectively threatening integrity. Advantage is relevant but not decisive by itself; it may arise unintentionally.
# 12.4 Cheating potential and critical incidents
An incident is critical only when it can distort competition one-sidedly or be deliberately exploited. The criteria are:
• It creates or could create a one-sided informational or decision advantage, or an illegal state enabling one.
• It could be used repeatedly or systematically as a strategy (cheating potential).
If these criteria are met, the Judge or management must record and forward the incident under chapter 15.
# 12.5 Infraction categories
Assign an incident to the most appropriate category. A more specific category takes precedence over a general one.
# 12.5.1 Game Rule Violation (GRV)
A GRV is an unintentional misapplication of a game rule or illegal performance of a mandatory action when no more specific category applies. Examples include incorrect action order, timing, priority or response opportunities, misapplied effects other than missed effects, incorrect zone placement and incorrect conditions or counters.
GRVs often involve GSC timing and the Reaction Chain. Denying an opponent a legal response opportunity, practically or formally, can be a GRV.
Do not use this category when Hidden Information Error, Deck/Decklist Error, Unsporting Conduct or Cheating fits better.
# 12.5.2 Missed Mandatory Effect / Missed Trigger
This occurs when a mandatory card-text or game-rule effect is not performed despite its event or condition occurring. It includes time-based triggers, mandatory ongoing conditions or degeneration, and other automatic or required processes.
The effect must be mandatory, its condition must have occurred, and its execution must have been omitted or attempted late enough to depart from the legal sequence.
Whether and how it can be resolved later follows chapter 14 and may depend on REL.
# 12.5.3 Hidden Information Error
This includes improper disclosure, viewing or influence of hidden information, or illegal handling of hidden areas. Examples include seeing cards that must remain hidden, incorrect draws, drawing extra cards, incorrect shuffling, and information acquired through a violation that the player would not normally have.
This category is sensitive because information often cannot be taken back.
# 12.5.4 Deck / Decklist Error
This includes discrepancies between a deck and required list, incomplete or illegible lists, illegal cards or quantities, illegal structure, and marked or distinguishable sleeves or cards.
In sanctioned events, the official Banned & Restricted list is bound to the event. Deviations from it are Deck/Decklist Errors (section 9.2).
# 12.5.5 Unsporting Conduct
Conduct or communication that undermines fair play without already constituting Cheating includes insults, aggression or intimidation, deliberate distraction, refusal to cooperate reasonably with officials, and abuse of processes such as Judge calls to disrupt or delay.
Avoidable slow play is also unsporting, particularly if repeated, used at decisive stages or recognisably intended to reach extra rounds. Treat avoidable delays under chapter 13.
# 12.5.6 Cheating
Cheating is deliberately breaking rules or knowingly using or creating false information to gain an advantage or disadvantage the opponent. Examples include deliberate rules violations, creating or exploiting illegal states, manipulating hidden information, false statements to officials, marking or manipulating cards, sleeves or decks, and other deception distorting competition.
Success is not required; an attempt or knowingly accepting the violation may suffice. Cheating is serious at every REL.
# 12.6 Multiple and related violations
Classify connected violations separately if they belong to different categories. One incident may receive multiple categories only when genuinely distinct violations exist.
Repeated same-type errors may indicate insufficient knowledge, carelessness or intent and must be considered under chapter 13.
# 13 Measures and penalties
This chapter covers tournament consequences of chapter 12 violations. Game-state corrections are covered separately in chapter 14.
Measures protect match and tournament integrity, equal treatment, prevent cheating potential and ensure consistent treatment of repeated or escalating incidents. Judges or management apply them according to classification and REL (section 7.3).
# 13.1 Application principles
Measures protect integrity and prevent repetition and cheating potential. They do not compensate for an in-game advantage and are not game corrections.
Apply them consistently, transparently and proportionately, considering extent, match and tournament impact, repetition, cooperation and cheating potential.
An error is not serious merely because it affects play; absence of intent does not automatically justify leniency. Objective harm to competition governs.
Critical incidents must be documented and forwarded under chapter 15 regardless of the measure imposed. The German source refers here to “12.1.4”; the critical-incident criteria are in section 12.4.
# 13.2 Available measures
Measures may be used individually or in combination according to the incident, REL and overall context. Not all are penalties: advice, organisational interventions and time measures ensure proper operation. Scoring consequences apply when fair match integrity cannot otherwise be assured. Officials exercise discretion under section 13.1.

1. Caution
A non-punitive explanation of correct rules application, possibly identifying the relevant rule. Appropriate for first minor errors, obvious misunderstandings without advantage potential, and learning or low-REL settings, particularly REL 0 or 1. Normally not recorded across events, but management may make an internal record.

2. Warning
A documented formal measure indicating that the incident exceeds a simple misunderstanding or that repetition may escalate. Appropriate when play or decisions were affected, the same or similar error repeats, a caution was already given, or greater care is expected, particularly at REL 2 or 3.

3. Upgrade / escalation
An increase in intervention because of repetition, severity or the overall situation, not an independent measure. Relevant when errors repeat after cautions or warnings, multiple similar errors occur, repetition structurally distorts competition or cheating potential increases.
Cautions can become Warnings, and Warnings can become scoring consequences. Disqualification is not automatic escalation; it must be justified by the overall severity and cheating potential.

4. Penalty
The affected Sovereign immediately loses 5 life points. This may cause a game loss but does not automatically end the match. Appropriate when more than a Warning is needed but Match Loss would be disproportionate, particularly for repeated errors or complex states with increased cheating potential.
At REL 1, use it only through the Caution → Warning → Penalty escalation. At REL 2 and above, it is a regular available measure. It is not intended at REL 0. It may escalate to Match Loss.

5. Match Loss
The most severe regular scoring consequence within an ongoing match. Use when cautions, warnings, penalties or corrections cannot preserve fair competition, or continued violations require escalation. Relevant to irreparable one-sided advantage, inability to restore a legal state or objective destruction of the basis of a fair match.
At REL 1 and above, it may follow Caution → Warning → Penalty → Match Loss after repeated opportunities to learn and correct. At REL 2 and above, it may be immediate if the criteria are met, correction is impossible or insufficient and a Penalty would be inadequate.

6. Disqualification
The most severe tournament measure, particularly for Cheating, serious unsporting conduct, irreparable deck/list errors or excessive abuse potential. It excludes the player from the current event regardless of previous results and must always be documented and forwarded under chapter 15.
Actual realised advantage is not required; attempts, knowing acceptance or planned exploitation may suffice. It is a last resort for substantial abuse potential, Cheating, or repeated unrepentant violations threatening integrity.
It applies only to participation in the sanctioned event. Whether the player must leave the area or venue is separately determined by the organiser's venue rights, independently of the tournament classification.
# 13.3 Application by Rule Enforcement Level
Typical availability:
• Caution: standard at REL 1, rare at REL 2 and 3.
• Warning: possible depending on the error at REL 1; standard at REL 2 and 3.
• Penalty: after a Warning at REL 1; regularly available at REL 2 and 3.
• Match Loss: after a Penalty at REL 1; after a Penalty, rarely direct, at REL 2; regularly available at REL 3.
• Disqualification: case by case at all three levels.

Typical measures by category (guidance, not rigid requirements):
GRV / missed mandatory effects / communication and procedural errors:
REL 1: Caution → Warning → Penalty → Match Loss. REL 2 and 3: Warning / Penalty → Match Loss.
Hidden Information Error:
REL 1: Warning → Penalty → Match Loss. REL 2 and 3: Penalty or Match Loss.
Deck / Decklist Error:
REL 1: Warning → Penalty → Match Loss. REL 2: Penalty → Match Loss, possibly DQ. REL 3: Match Loss, possibly DQ.
Unsporting Conduct without Cheating:
REL 1: Warning → Penalty → Match Loss. REL 2 and 3: Warning / Penalty / Match Loss.
Cheating: Disqualification at all three levels.

Penalty means −5 life points. The source table calls REL 3 “Professional”, while section 7.3 calls it “Competitive”.
# 14 Error correction
This chapter addresses game-state corrections only. Whether a measure under chapter 13 also applies is assessed independently. Restore a legal and fair state without creating new imbalance or informational advantage.
# 14.1 Correction principles
Fairness before perfection: aim for a fair, playable state, not a perfect reconstruction of a hypothetical legal sequence.
Minimal intervention: intervene as little as possible. The longer ago the error and the more later decisions, the more cautious a backup should be.
No new informational advantage: do not create additional information or allow retrospective decisions using knowledge unavailable in legal play.
Pause immediately: take no further actions until a Judge or management resolves the issue.
Official decision: corrections are ordered by a Judge or management. Players may correct only obvious, unambiguous errors with no hidden-information impact, by mutual agreement. At REL 2 and above, corrections should generally involve a Judge.
# 14.2 Procedure when an error is found
1. Pause play.
2. Establish facts: which rules were violated, when, which cards, counters or zones are affected, and what happened before discovery.
3. Determine effects: identify the illegal state and decisions made on its basis.
4. Choose a correction. If section 14.1 permits, back up to the last clearly legal state. If reconstruction is impossible or unhelpful, correct only the present state if it is a GRV. Resolve missed mandatory triggers if legally possible, fully and in the required order, without creating improper one-sided informational or decision advantages.
5. Carry out the correction.
6. Establish an unambiguous state.
Add time needed for a Judge's correction under section 10.4.1. The Judge determines the extent under section 14.1.
# 14.3 Backup
A backup reverses actions to restore an earlier state. It is allowed only when the sequence is clearly reconstructable, few later actions occurred, hidden-information changes are absent or controllable, and no new advantage results.
It is generally unsuitable after cards were drawn or searched for, decks shuffled or random effects performed, multiple hidden-information decisions made, or late discovery. Prefer direct state correction in these cases.
# 14.4 Corrections by category
These are typical guidelines for chapter 12 categories, not a substitute for case-specific assessment.
# 14.4.1 Game Rule Violation (GRV)
Typical corrections:
• Set, adjust or remove incorrect markers and conditions.
• Move misplaced cards to the correct zone or Lane; identify the correct active Lane.
• Determine current values correctly after misapplied modifiers. Existing damage remains unless safe reconstruction is possible.
• Reverse an incorrect play with no further consequences, for example playing a level 2 Character with only a level 1 Resource.
Prefer direct correction over a backup when no hidden-information intervention is required.
# 14.4.2 Missed mandatory effects
Mandatory effects, such as degenerative conditions, offer no choice whether to trigger. Correction depends particularly on how much time has passed.
Prompt discovery, normally in the same round: resolve the effect later if execution is clearly reconstructable. If required decisions about modes, targets or order can be made legally without affecting the game state, assume they were made accordingly.
Late discovery: do not completely replay the effect retrospectively. Resume legal play at the next possible correct point.
A correction must not allow decisions to be remade or changed using newly learned information.
# 14.4.3 Hidden Information Errors
Information already gained cannot be undone. Reduce asymmetry and restore randomness where possible instead of pretending to erase knowledge.
After accidentally viewing cards, do not give that player new decisions about selecting, ordering or replacing those cards unless such a choice was already legal. Where possible, randomise the affected hidden area so knowledge does not create an additional structural advantage.

A) Accidentally seeing deck contents
If one card is seen and the deck is otherwise random with no known order information, shuffle the deck and offer the opponent a cut.
If shuffling would erase legally known information, such as a known bottom card or a card placed at a defined position, do not automatically randomise. Stabilise the state without creating new choices; the Judge decides under section 14.1.

B) Drawing too many cards
If the extra cards can be unambiguously identified, return them to the correct zone. If their original deck position cannot be determined, shuffle the deck and offer a cut.
If extra cards cannot be identified, do not back up. Correct only as far as possible without further asymmetry, for example by restoring the proper hand size through random selection. Exercise particular care because of the high abuse potential.

C) Incorrectly revealing hand cards
Correction is relevant only when revealing was based on an incorrect assumption that a rule or effect required it. The information cannot be erased. Correct the assumption and continue legally. Reveal no additional cards and allow no additional decisions based on the disclosure.
Voluntary, deliberate or careless disclosure, such as dropped or accidentally visible cards, does not require a game correction. Continue unchanged.
# 14.4.4 Deck / Decklist Errors
Keep the deck clearly legal throughout the event. Typical corrections:
• Deck differs from list: adjust the deck to match, then randomise.
• Obvious writing or naming error: correct under chapters 12 and 13 if unambiguous.
• Card in the wrong Pool or Spell Deck: move it correctly and randomise both decks.
• Marked or damaged sleeves or cards: replace or standardise sleeves, then randomise.
• Wrong card count despite agreement with the list: remove excess cards; if short, add standard Resource cards usable in either deck, such as Priest or Smith.
• Illegal list under format, bans, size or rarity limits: compare with the actual deck and correct the list if the deck is legal.
At REL 2 and above, severity may justify Disqualification. Investigate carefully.
# 14.4.5 Communication and procedural errors
These concern unclear or insufficient communication about actions, targets, conditions or Lane assignments.
The Judge questions those involved to establish an objectively understandable state. Reverse decisions only as far as necessary to establish a legal state, and instruct players on clear communication and resolution.
Deliberately unclear communication, or communication capable of persistently disrupting play or the event, may also be Unsporting Conduct.
# 14.4.6 Distinguishing Cheating and Unsporting Conduct
Corrections do not remedy Cheating, but identifying an error can help detect it. If Cheating or serious Unsporting Conduct is suspected, pause play and follow chapters 13 and 15.
# 15 Appeals, documentation and Cheating procedures
# 15.1 Purpose and scope
This chapter governs appeals, records of tournament incidents and investigations of suspected Cheating. It supplements chapter 12 categories, chapter 13 measures and chapter 14 corrections with procedures, particularly rights and duties when integrity may be affected beyond the immediate game situation.
# 15.2 Appeals
Only a directly affected player in the match may appeal a Judge's rules or procedural decision. Spectators and players in other matches may not.
Appeals may challenge interpretation, infraction classification under chapter 12 or measures under chapter 13. Purely organisational decisions affecting everyone equally, such as timing, seating, pairings and round operation, are not appealable.
# 15.2.1 Appeal procedure
Appeal immediately after the decision is announced, verbally to the Judge who made it. The Judge forwards it to the appeal authority.
Pause the match during the appeal and add the time under section 10.4.1.
# 15.2.2 Appeal authority
At REL 2 and above, appoint a Head Judge, the highest rules authority, who hears appeals against other Judges. The Head Judge may also observe matches and make rulings directly. Their decisions cannot be further appealed.
At REL 0 or 1, management acts as the final appeal authority unless a Head Judge is appointed.
The appeal authority reviews independently, is not bound by the initial assessment, and may confirm, modify or overturn it.
# 15.2.3 Effect and binding force
An appeal does not suspend the original decision unless the appeal authority expressly orders a temporary suspension.
The appeal decision is final and binding for the event and cannot be appealed again. Implement resulting game consequences immediately where consistent with chapter 14.
# 15.3 Incident documentation
# 15.3.1 Documentation requirements
Management must document incidents to make decisions reviewable and support consistent application beyond a single event. Required records include:
• Warnings, Penalties, Match Losses and Disqualifications.
• Critical incidents under section 12.4.
• Appeals and outcomes under section 15.2.
• Every investigated Cheating suspicion, regardless of outcome.
At REL 0 or 1, records may be limited to Disqualifications, suspected Cheating and critical incidents. At REL 2 and above, all listed records are mandatory.
# 15.3.2 Record contents
Include at least event name, date and REL; round and match number where applicable; players and software player IDs where available; understandable factual account; chapter 12 category; chapter 13 measure; chapter 14 correction if applicable; deciding official's name; and any appeal outcome.
Keep records factual, concise and limited to tournament-relevant facts.
# 15.3.3 Responsibility and retention
Management is responsible for completeness and accuracy even if recording is delegated to Judges.
Use the official software's available recording functions. Add handwritten or electronic records where the software is insufficient. Retain and archive according to Bluthelden® Organized Play requirements.
# 15.3.4 Forwarding to Organized Play
Promptly send documented incidents to Bluthelden® Organized Play after the event, using the contact methods, such as email, specified by Organized Play.
# 15.4 Investigating suspected Cheating
# 15.4.1 Principle
Protect tournament integrity and players against unjust accusations through a careful, fair process. Cheating findings are serious and may have far-reaching consequences.
All elements in section 12.5.6 must be present together: intent, a rules violation and an actual or attempted advantage.
# 15.4.2 Starting an investigation
Investigate when a Judge, management or player raises a specific, reasoned suspicion of a deliberate violation under section 12.5.6.
A Judge or management investigates. At REL 2 and above, involve the Head Judge where possible. Immediately pause the match and add the investigation time to the round allowance. Both players must cooperate and answer truthfully.
# 15.4.3 Establishing facts and hearing players
Establish events as fully as possible using objective evidence and statements. In particular:
• Interview players separately so neither hears or responds to the other's account.
• Preserve and inspect card positions, markers, conditions, life points and Graveyard contents.
• Examine decks, lists, sleeves and other materials where relevant.
• Interview uninvolved witnesses if available.
• Check records of similar incidents at this or other events.
Both players may fully present their perspective before a decision. Do not disclose hidden game information or improperly reveal the other player's account during questioning.
# 15.4.4 Assessment standard
All three elements are required: a violation of card text or game/tournament rules; knowing, deliberate conduct or conscious acceptance of the violation; and an actual or attempted advantage for the player or disadvantage for the opponent.
The standard is clear conviction: the investigating authority must be highly confident from the evidence that all three are present. Mere suspicion, advantage alone or theoretical intent is insufficient.
Resolve doubt in the accused player's favour. If Cheating cannot be established with clear conviction, classify and handle the incident under the appropriate other category in chapter 12.
# 15.4.5 Decision and measures
If clearly convinced of Cheating, classify under section 12.5.6 and Disqualify under section 13.2, regardless of REL.
Otherwise do not classify as Cheating. Apply the appropriate chapter 12 category and the measure for that category and REL.
Regardless of outcome, document every investigated suspicion under section 15.3 and forward it under section 15.3.4.
Inform the accused player of the decision and reasons. After a Cheating Disqualification, explain that Organized Play will receive the incident and may impose consequences across events.
An appeal under section 15.2 is available unless the finding was already made by the Head Judge or management acting as the appeal authority.
# 15.4.6 Protection against false accusations
Do not handle Cheating accusations carelessly or abusively. Deliberately accusing while knowing, or when one should recognise, that grounds are insufficient may constitute serious Unsporting Conduct under section 12.5.5. The same applies to repeated misuse for pressure, intimidation or delay.
Officials must treat the accused fairly and respectfully. Suspicion is not prejudgment; preserve the benefit of doubt until a final finding.
# 15.5 Consequences across tournaments
# 15.5.1 Principle
Confirmed Cheating, Disqualifications and repeated serious violations across events may have broader consequences. These protect integrity and equal conditions throughout Bluthelden® Organized Play.
# 15.5.2 Responsibility
Only Bluthelden® Organized Play decides consequences across tournaments. Local management's role is to document accurately and forward incidents under section 15.3.4. It does not decide bans, suspensions or other cross-event measures.
# 15.5.3 Possible measures across tournaments
Possible measures include temporary or indefinite exclusion from sanctioned events, removal of Organized Play points or qualifications, and exclusion from invitation or special events.
Organized Play determines requirements, severity levels, duration, procedures and possible rehabilitation.
# 15.5.4 Reference
Bluthelden® Organized Play alone is responsible for consequences across tournaments. This rulebook creates no independent cross-event entitlement or measure; it ensures the necessary information is properly recorded and forwarded.
